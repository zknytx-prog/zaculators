Debt-to-Income Ratio Calculator

What This Calculator Estimates

Debt-to-income (DTI) ratio compares your total monthly debt payments to your gross monthly income, widely used by lenders to assess borrowing capacity and risk, with lower ratios generally viewed more favorably for loan approval, particularly for mortgages. This calculator estimates your DTI ratio using your total monthly debt payments and gross income, helping you understand your position relative to common lending guidelines.

Formula / Method Used

The method is straightforward: monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income, multiplied by 100. The calculator also assigns a simple planning label based on the resulting percentage so you can quickly see whether the ratio appears lower, moderate, or higher.

Worked Example

If monthly debt payments total $1,500 and gross monthly income is $5,000, the DTI is 30%. Under this page's simplified labeling, that would appear as a moderate ratio because debt is taking up about three tenths of monthly gross income.

How to Interpret the Result

A lower ratio generally leaves more room in the budget for new obligations, while a higher ratio suggests tighter cash flow. The estimate is best used as a screening tool before comparing lender standards, not as a final approval decision.

Common Mistakes

  • Using net pay instead of gross monthly income.
  • Leaving out minimum payments on credit cards or installment loans.
  • Including one-time bills that are not part of recurring debt obligations.
  • Assuming every lender will use the same DTI limit.

Tips for a More Accurate Estimate

  • Include all recurring debt obligations, not just your largest ones, for an accurate DTI figure.
  • Pay down existing debt before a major loan application to improve your DTI ratio.
  • Avoid taking on new debt in the months before applying for a mortgage or major loan.
  • Check specific lender DTI guidelines for the loan type you're pursuing, since thresholds vary.
  • Recalculate as your income or debt obligations change over time.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is considered a good debt-to-income ratio?

Lenders generally view lower DTI ratios more favorably, with specific thresholds varying by loan type and lender — mortgage lenders in particular often reference specific DTI guidelines as part of their approval criteria.

What debts are typically included in DTI calculation?

Common inclusions are mortgage or rent, car loans, student loans, credit card minimum payments, and other recurring debt obligations — though specific lender definitions can vary slightly.

Does DTI include expenses like groceries or utilities?

No, DTI specifically measures debt obligations, not general living expenses like groceries, utilities, or insurance, even though those also affect your overall budget.

How can I improve my debt-to-income ratio?

Paying down existing debt, avoiding new debt, and/or increasing income are the primary ways to improve DTI ratio, each directly affecting either the numerator or denominator of the calculation.

Why do lenders care about DTI ratio?

DTI ratio helps lenders assess how much of your income is already committed to debt obligations, giving insight into your capacity to take on and repay additional debt responsibly.
