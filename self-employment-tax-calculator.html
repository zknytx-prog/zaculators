What This Calculator Estimates

Self-employment tax (in the US, covering Social Security and Medicare contributions) applies to net self-employment income, and because self-employed individuals act as both 'employer' and 'employee,' they generally pay both halves of these contributions rather than splitting them with an employer as traditional employees do. Because rates, thresholds, and rules vary by country for self-employed individuals, this calculator lets you enter your applicable rate to estimate the contribution on your net self-employment earnings.

Formula / Method Used

  • Estimated self-employment tax = Net self-employment income x Tax rate
  • Estimated income after self-employment tax = Net self-employment income - Estimated self-employment tax

Worked Example

If net self-employment income is $80,000 and the tax rate is 15.3%, the estimated self-employment tax is $12,240. The amount left after that tax is about $67,760.

Common Mistakes

  • Entering gross revenue instead of net self-employment income.
  • Assuming self-employment tax is the same as total tax due.
  • Ignoring thresholds, caps, deductions, or deductible portions of the tax.
  • Using last year's rate assumptions after rules change.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do self-employed people pay more in payroll-style taxes than employees?

Traditional employees split Social Security/Medicare (or equivalent) contributions with their employer, while self-employed individuals are responsible for both the 'employee' and 'employer' portions, effectively doubling the headline contribution rate.

Is self-employment tax the same as income tax?

No, self-employment tax specifically covers Social Security and Medicare-style contributions (in the US) and is separate from regular income tax owed on your profits.

Can self-employed individuals deduct part of this tax?

In the US, self-employed individuals can typically deduct half of their self-employment tax when calculating adjusted gross income — check current IRS rules for exact treatment.

Does this apply only to US self-employed individuals?

The specific 'self-employment tax' terminology is US-focused, but similar concepts (higher social security contributions for self-employed workers) exist in many countries — enter the rate applicable to your jurisdiction.

Where can I confirm current official rates and thresholds?

In the US, the IRS publishes current self-employment tax rates and the wage base limit for the Social Security portion; other countries' tax or social security authorities publish their equivalents.
